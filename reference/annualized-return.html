How annualized rate of return works.

Go to Lesson 05: Cash Conversion Cycle →

The Speedometer Rule: If you drive 5 miles in 6 minutes, you traveled at 50 miles per hour—even though you didn't drive for a whole hour. An annualized return does the exact same thing for money: it scales any short-term or long-term profit into a standard 1-year rate so you can compare completely different opportunities side by side.

1. The basic formula, step by step

To annualize any return, you only need three numbers:

  1. Dollar gain: The actual profit or cash saved (e.g. $80 discount).
  2. Cash put at risk (principal): The actual cash you had to pay out (e.g. $3,920 cash paid).
  3. Holding period: The number of days the money was committed or pulled forward (e.g. 15 days).

Step 1: Find the period return (the raw percentage)

What percentage did you make on the cash you actually laid out?

Period Return = Dollar Gain ÷ Cash Invested = $80 ÷ $3,920 ≈ 0.020408 (2.0408%)

Notice: You divide by $3,920 (the cash that left your bank), not $4,000. You didn't invest $4,000; you only paid $3,920 to keep $80 in your pocket.

Step 2: Find the annualization multiplier

How many 15-day intervals exist in a 365-day year?

Periods per Year = 365 days ÷ Holding Days = 365 ÷ 15 ≈ 24.333 times per year

Step 3: Multiply to get Simple Annualized Return (APR)

If you could repeat this exact transaction back-to-back all year without compounding:

Simple Annualized Return = Period Return × (365 ÷ Days) = 2.0408% × 24.333 ≈ 49.66%

2. Simple vs. Compounded (APR vs. APY)

Finance has two ways to quote an annualized number. It helps to understand both:

3. Worked examples and corner cases

Example A: The classic trade credit rule ("2/10 net 30")

A supplier terms invoice states "2/10 net 30": pay in 10 days to get a 2% discount; otherwise pay the full bill on day 30.

  • Days accelerated: 30 − 10 = 20 days.
  • Raw return: $2 ÷ $98 = 2.041%.
  • Simple annualized rate: 2.041% × (365 ÷ 20) = 37.24%.
  • Business insight: Skipping this discount is economically equivalent to borrowing money from your supplier at a 37.2% annual interest rate!

Corner Case 1: Ultra-short periods (The 1-day or 3-day trap)

"Pay 2 days early and save 1%!"

  • Raw return: $1 ÷ $99 = 1.01%.
  • Periods per year: 365 ÷ 2 = 182.5.
  • Simple annualized rate: 1.01% × 182.5 = 184.3%!
  • The Trap: Annualizing short windows creates astronomical percentages. But you cannot actually perform that transaction 182 times a year. Always check the absolute dollars ($10 or $10,000?) and administrative transaction costs before getting excited by a 180% rate.

Corner Case 2: Long multi-year horizons (When Days > 365)

Suppose Cedar invested $10,000 in a new automated saw, and 3 years later (1,095 days) sold it for $16,000.

  • Total dollar gain: $6,000 (60% cumulative gain).
  • The Beginner Mistake: 60% ÷ 3 = 20% per year (this ignores compounding).
  • The Correct Annualized Rate (CAGR):
    Annualized Return (CAGR) = (1 + 0.60)(365 ÷ 1,095) − 1 = (1.60)1/3 − 1 = 1.1696 − 1 = 16.96% per year

Corner Case 3: The Base Denominator Trap (Gross vs. Net)

A frequent error is dividing the $80 discount by the original $4,000 invoice price ($80 ÷ $4,000 = 2.000%).

  • Why this is wrong: Cedar never paid $4,000. It paid $3,920. If you hand over $3,920 and pocket an extra $80 compared to waiting, the return on the cash you committed is $80 ÷ $3,920 = 2.041%.
  • Rule: Always divide gain by the cash disbursed, never by the nominal invoice total.

Corner Case 4: Negative Returns (Losses)

If an investment loses 4% over 60 days:

  • Simple annualized loss: −4% × (365 ÷ 60) = −24.33%.
  • Boundary condition: In compounding, you cannot lose more than −100% (total ruin). If an asset hits zero, the return is −100%, period.

Corner Case 5: Zero Days Elapsed (The Division by Zero Error)

If you negotiate an immediate discount at point of sale on a purchase you were already paying for today:

  • Days elapsed = 0.
  • Periods per year: 365 ÷ 0 = Undefined.
  • This is not an investment return over time; it is an immediate reduction in purchase price (lower inventory cost).

4. Agent-world design summary

Three rules for business reasoning agents:
  1. Compare APR to the cost of borrowing: If Cedar can borrow cash from a bank line of credit at 8% annual interest, paying early to capture a 37% or 49% annualized discount is profitable—borrow at 8% to earn 49%.
  2. Never evaluate annualized return without a liquidity check: An agent that optimizes APR without checking minimum cash will happily capture an $80 discount today and bounce payroll tomorrow.
  3. Watch out for friction: Small dollar savings on short periods rarely justify wire transfer fees, extra invoice processing, or staff overhead.
