Put money on a calendar.

Cash after an event = cash before it + receipts − payments.
Track each event on its date. A positive final balance does not prove that every earlier payment can be made.

Cedar's isolated batch

Opening cash $5,000, no other assets or obligations. Day 0: receive $4,000 inventory, due day 20. Day 10: sell and deliver for $6,000, customer pays day 40. No other transactions in the baseline.

At each date, add cash + inventory + receivable to check total assets; compare with payable + equity. Day-10 profit is $6,000 revenue − $4,000 cost of goods sold = $2,000. No other income or expenses, interest, taxes, or inventory losses are assumed.

Useful terms and checks

  • Cost of goods sold: the carrying cost of inventory sold, recognized as expense with its related revenue in this example.
  • Payment terms: agreed conditions governing when and how payment is due. A due date is not a guarantee of actual collection.
  • Cash forecast: an estimate of dated receipts, payments, and resulting balances. Separate contractual due dates, expected dates, and actual dates.
  • Funding gap: cash needed to meet a payment beyond the cash available at that time. Compute from the schedule, not profit alone.

Agent checklist: identify event and counterparty → update inventory/receivable/payable → put cash on its expected date → recalculate balances → test delayed receipts → escalate gaps or obtain agreed changes before committing. Do not silently defer a payment past its due date.

Primary sources: IAS 7: operating cash receipts/payments versus profit; IFRS 15: revenue recognition; IAS 2: inventory expense recognition. The calendar and forecasting checklist are teaching applications, not claims that these standards prescribe an agent workflow.
