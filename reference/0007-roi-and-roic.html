Capital efficiency & returns.

Go to Lesson 07: Returns on Capital (ROI & ROIC) →

The Capital Return Rule:
Profit alone is an incomplete measure of success. A business creates value only when the profit it earns exceeds the cost of the capital tied up to generate it.
ROIC = Operating Profit (after tax) ÷ Invested Capital
ROI (Project level) = (Net Gain − Cost of Investment) ÷ Cost of Investment

Core capital return metrics

MetricScopeCalculationKey question it answers
ROIC
Return on Invested Capital		 Entire company or operating division Operating Profit ÷ Invested Capital
(or NOPAT ÷ [Equity + Debt − Cash])		 For every $1 of capital provided by investors and lenders, how many cents of operating profit did management generate?
ROI
Return on Investment		 Specific project, equipment purchase, or ad campaign Net Financial Gain ÷ Total Cost of Investment Did this specific expenditure generate more cash than it cost to undertake?
Capital Turnover
Asset Turnover		 Capital velocity Revenue ÷ Invested Capital How many dollars of sales does the company generate for every $1 of capital tied up?
Cost of Capital (WACC / Hurdle Rate) Financing threshold Weighted Average Cost of Debt & Equity What is the minimum percentage return investors and lenders demand? If ROIC < Cost of Capital, value is destroyed.

The DuPont Equation: The two ways to generate high returns

ROIC = Operating Margin × Capital Turnover
(Operating Profit ÷ Capital) = (Operating Profit ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Capital)
StrategyOperating MarginCapital TurnoverReal-world business archetype
High-Margin Route High (e.g. 25% – 40%) Low (turns capital 0.8x – 1.2x a year) Bespoke furniture makers, luxury goods, enterprise software, consulting firms.
High-Turnover Route Slim (e.g. 3% – 6%) High (turns capital 5x – 10x a year) Flat-pack desk makers, supermarkets, discount retailers (Costco, Walmart, Amazon).

Departmental drivers of capital on the balance sheet

DepartmentBalance Sheet Asset tied upEfficiency leversDanger of over-optimization
Operations Factory machinery, assembly tools (CapEx) Maximize machine uptime, preventive maintenance, multi-purpose tooling Buying over-automated machines for low-volume products freezes capital
Procurement Raw materials & components inventory Just-in-time delivery, consignment inventory, smaller batch sizes Running out of stock halts assembly lines and delays customer shipments
Sales & Credit Accounts receivable (uncollected invoices) Strict credit reviews, prompt payment incentives, upfront deposits Excessively harsh payment terms drive buyers to more accommodating competitors
IT Computer hardware, servers, enterprise software Cloud SaaS (OpEx) vs. on-premise hardware purchases (CapEx) Underinvesting in IT security or infrastructure causes downtime and technical debt

Agent-world decision checklist for capital allocation

  • Check the Hurdle Rate: Never approve a project just because its dollar return is positive. Verify that Project ROI > Cost of Capital.
  • Dollar Profit vs. Capital Efficiency: An initiative requiring $100,000 to produce $12,000 profit (12% ROIC) is far worse than an initiative requiring $10,000 to produce $4,000 profit (40% ROIC) when debt financing costs 8%.
  • Look at Working Capital: Capital isn't just machines; every extra day of inventory (DIO) or uncollected receivables (DSO) ties up cash on the balance sheet that requires financing.
