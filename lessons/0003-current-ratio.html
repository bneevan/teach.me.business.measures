Business worlds for AI agents / Lesson 03 / 6 minutes

A ratio is not a payment plan.

Your win: calculate current ratio, then explain why it cannot guarantee tomorrow's bills will be paid.

First: rebuild the total

After Cedar pays $1,000 of its existing supplier bill, cash is $13,000, inventory $6,000, liabilities $6,000, and equity $13,000. There are no other assets.

What are total assets now?

Which resources and obligations are short-term?

Current assets include cash and resources expected to be converted to cash or used up in the normal operating cycle or within a year. Current liabilities are short-term obligations, such as supplier bills and debt due within a year. The operating cycle is the business's journey from acquiring inputs through selling and collecting payment. These are introductory descriptions, not every accounting classification rule. [1, 2]

For Cedar, assume an operating cycle shorter than a year, unrestricted cash, inventory expected to sell within three months, and all $6,000 of liabilities due within twelve months. Thus all assets and liabilities in this particular snapshot are current. Do not assume that for every company.

The current ratio

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities [3]
Cedar: ($13,000 cash + $6,000 inventory) ÷ $6,000 = 3.17:1, rounded.

Interpretation: Cedar has about $3.17 of recorded current assets for every $1 of current liabilities. This is not $3.17 of cash, not a profit margin, and not a forecast of when money arrives. It is a snapshot based on reported balances. [3]

What does Cedar's 3.17:1 ratio compare?

Procurement can change cash without changing the ratio

Suppose Cedar buys another $10,000 of unsold resale inventory with cash. Assume it qualifies as current inventory, is worth at least cost, and there are no other changes.

MeasureBefore purchaseAfter purchase
Cash$13,000$3,000
Inventory$6,000$16,000
Current assets$19,000$19,000
Current liabilities$6,000$6,000
Current ratio3.17:13.17:1

The ratio is unchanged because cash became inventory. But the business now has less money available immediately. If $4,000 of its existing liabilities is due tomorrow and there are no incoming receipts or other funding, $3,000 cash will not cover that payment. This limitation follows directly from the example: a total does not tell us its composition or timing.

Connect the departments—and the agents

Procurement buys the desks; Operations stores them; Sales must find buyers; Finance tracks when cash arrives and bills fall due. Buying inventory may be worthwhile, but a current-ratio target alone cannot decide that.

Agent guardrail: before authorizing the purchase, check a dated cash forecast and upcoming obligations, not just the ratio. There is no universal best ratio: interpretation needs business context, asset quality, timing, and comparable companies. BDC provides a general benchmark; do not turn it into an automatic rule for every industry. [3]

Tell your teacher: After the inventory purchase, a teammate says, “Our current ratio is still 3.17, so tomorrow's $4,000 bill is safe.” Do you agree? State the cash shortfall and explain what the ratio misses.
Ask the agent follow-up questions about classification, the arithmetic, or how different departments influence cash.
