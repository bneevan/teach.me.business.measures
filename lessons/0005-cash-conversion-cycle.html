Quick reference: Cash conversion cycle ↗

Recall before we add anything

Cedar pays its supplier on day 20 and collects from its customer on day 40. During that 20-day gap, what funds the company?

From a dated timeline to three standard measures

In Lesson 04, Cedar bought desks on credit on day 0, delivered them on credit on day 10, paid the supplier on day 20, and collected from the customer on day 40. Corporate finance standardizes those intervals into three working capital measures: [1]

The Cash Conversion Cycle formula

The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) measures the net time (in days) between paying for goods and collecting cash from customers. It expresses the gap that requires external funding: [1]

The CCC Formula:
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO

Cedar’s baseline: 10 days (inventory) + 30 days (receivable) − 20 days (payable) = 20 days

During those 20 days (from day 20 to day 40), Cedar has sent $4,000 of cash to the supplier but has received nothing yet from the customer. Cedar's cash is tied up in working capital. A shorter cycle means cash returns to the bank faster.

If a company has 15 days DIO, 30 days DSO, and 25 days DPO, what is its cash conversion cycle?

The trade-offs: why you cannot squeeze CCC to zero naively

Every department has levers to alter the cycle, but aggressive single-metric optimization creates immediate operational risks:

Department & LeverAction to shorten CCCHidden danger or trade-off
Operations (DIO)Slash inventory holding levels to the bare minimum.Stockouts: Cedar runs out of desks, delaying deliveries, disappointing customers, and losing sales.
Sales (DSO)Demand upfront cash or enforce rigid 7-day payment terms.Customer loss: B2B buyers switch to competitors offering flexible 30-day or 60-day terms.
Procurement (DPO)Unilaterally stretch payments to suppliers out to 60 days.Supplier conflict: suppliers halt deliveries, add financing penalties, or refuse volume discounts.

Negative CCC: when suppliers fund your business

When DPO exceeds the entire operating cycle (DPO > DIO + DSO), the cash conversion cycle becomes negative. Retailers and grocery chains frequently achieve this: customers pay instantly at checkout (DSO ≈ 0), inventory sells in 5 days (DIO = 5), while suppliers are paid on 45-day terms (DPO = 45). The resulting CCC is −40 days—meaning suppliers provide free operational financing before the company pays a single dollar. [1]

Agent-world rules and guardrails

Design guardrail for business agents:
  1. Never give an agent a single-metric CCC objective. An automated procurement agent maximizing DPO will destroy vendor trust; an automated sales agent minimizing DSO will reject profitable credit customers.
  2. Model explicit constraints: bound DIO by a safety-stock threshold, bound DSO by market competitive terms, and bound DPO by contractual credit agreements.
  3. Calculate dollar cost of the cycle: A 20-day CCC on $10,000 daily sales locks up $200,000 of cash. If that cash must be borrowed at 8% annual interest, the cycle carries an explicit financing expense.
