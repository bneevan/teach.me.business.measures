Returns on capital:
ROI and ROIC.

Your win: connect the income statement to the balance sheet, calculate ROI and ROIC, and discover why higher profit dollars do not always mean a better business.

Quick reference: ROI & ROIC ↗

Recall before we add anything

Cedar has a 40% Gross Margin. An ad campaign costs $4,000. How much new sales revenue must it generate just to break even on operating profit?

The missing dimension: Profit without capital is half the story

In Lesson 06, we looked at margins: how many cents of profit a company keeps from each dollar of revenue. But profit margin alone cannot tell you if a business is successful. You must also ask: how much capital had to be tied up on the balance sheet to produce that profit? [1]

Compare two desk workshops:
  • Workshop A (Hand tools): Generates $10,000 annual profit on $100,000 sales (10% net margin). It operates with just $20,000 of invested capital (basic hand saws and wood inventory). Its return on capital is 50% ($10,000 ÷ $20,000).
  • Workshop B (Robotic factory): Generates the same $10,000 annual profit on $100,000 sales (10% net margin). But it had to buy $500,000 of automated machinery. Its return on capital is only 2% ($10,000 ÷ $500,000).

Both businesses have the exact same revenue, profit, and margin. But Workshop A is an extraordinary wealth creator, while Workshop B earns less than a government bond!

ROI vs. ROIC: Project level vs. Company level

Corporate finance uses two primary metrics to connect profit back to capital: [2]

MetricLevelFormulaWhat it measures
ROI
Return on Investment		 Single initiative, project, or asset purchase (Net Gain − Cost of Investment) ÷ Cost of Investment The percentage gain generated by a specific project (e.g. buying a new forklift or launching an ad campaign).
ROIC
Return on Invested Capital		 Entire company or operating division Operating Profit (after tax) ÷ Invested Capital How efficiently the entire business allocates capital raised from debt and equity holders to generate operating profit.
What is "Invested Capital"?
Invested capital is the actual money tied up in running the business. On the balance sheet, it is typically:
Invested Capital = Fixed Assets (Machinery & Buildings) + Working Capital (Inventory + Receivables − Payables)

Equivalently: Shareholder Equity + Long-Term Debt − Excess Cash.

If Cedar earns $30,000 annual operating profit using $150,000 of invested capital, what is its ROIC?

Two ways to win: Margin vs. Turnover

A famous financial insight (the DuPont framework) shows that return on capital is the product of two distinct business engines: [2]

Return on Capital = Operating Margin × Capital Turnover
(Profit ÷ Capital) = (Profit ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Capital)

Every business model succeeds through one of two strategies:

  1. The High-Margin Route (Luxury & Custom): Sell bespoke, handcrafted desks at a 30% margin. Sales volume and capital turnover are low, but each sale yields high profit.
  2. The High-Turnover Route (Volume & Velocity): Sell standard flat-pack desks at a slim 5% margin. But cycle inventory, collect invoices rapidly, and turn over capital 6 times a year (5% × 6 = 30% ROIC). This is the secret of Amazon, Walmart, and Costco.

Departmental drivers of invested capital

Departments do not just spend money on the P&L; they tie up capital on the balance sheet:

DepartmentBalance Sheet Asset tied upCapital efficiency lever
Operations & Production Factory machinery, tools, warehouse space (CapEx) Machine utilization, preventive maintenance, leasing vs. buying.
Procurement & Logistics Raw materials and finished goods inventory (DIO) Lean inventory, just-in-time delivery, consignment stock.
Sales & Credit Accounts receivable (DSO) Prompt customer collections, credit limits, upfront deposits.
IT & Infrastructure Servers and enterprise software licenses Cloud SaaS (OpEx) vs. on-premise hardware purchases (CapEx).

Agent-world guardrails: The CapEx blind spot

Why agents fail when evaluating capital:
  1. The Automation Trap: An autonomous Operations agent tasked with "reducing unit production labor" might approve a $200,000 robotic sander. Unit production costs drop, but $200,000 of cash is now frozen in steel and motors. If sales volume is modest, the return on that $200,000 will be terrible.
  2. The Cost of Capital Hurdle: Capital is never free. If Cedar borrows money at 8% interest (or investors demand an 8% return), any project earning less than 8% destroys value, even if it is technically profitable in dollars!

Your turn: The custom tabletop dilemma

Tell your teacher: Cedar is deciding between two ways to expand production of custom oak conference tables:
  • Option A (Handcrafted / Low Capital): Buy $10,000 in specialized hand tools. Generates $4,000 in annual Operating Profit.
  • Option B (Automated CNC / High Capital): Buy a $100,000 computer-controlled carving machine. Generates $12,000 in annual Operating Profit.
  1. Calculate the ROIC (Operating Profit ÷ Capital) for Option A and Option B.
  2. Which option generates more absolute profit in dollars? Which option is a more efficient use of capital?
  3. Suppose Cedar must fund the equipment by taking out a bank loan at an 8% annual interest rate:
    • How much annual interest does Option A pay, and what is its profit after interest?
    • How much annual interest does Option B pay, and what is its profit after interest?
    • Which option leaves the owners with more remaining profit in their pocket?
Ask the agent anything unclear about return on capital, CapEx, or financing costs.
